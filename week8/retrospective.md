Retrospective: To the Person I Was in Week 1

Hey, it's you, a few months later. Back in Week 1, all you really knew was that this was "an AI fluency track", something about AI tools and learning. That was the whole picture. You didn't know it would end with a live website with a real working backend, a documented agent with actual guardrails, or a proof statement built around a real project you can point to when someone asks what you can do.

What you set out to do was learn to use AI tools. What actually happened was learning to work *with* AI as a partner who pushes back, not a vending machine that hands you finished answers. That distinction wasn't obvious in Week 1. It became obvious the first time Claude pressure-tested your portfolio sitemap and told you, plainly, that "About" was a detour standing between your strongest page and your one action. It became obvious again when the ML Research Scout agent kept honestly reporting "nothing found" instead of padding its answer with old news dressed up as new, and you had to sit with that and decide whether the honest emptiness was actually more valuable than a fake-full brief. It was.

If I had to name what changed most concretely, it's this: I write specs before I build now. My final year project, TrafficSmart, mostly got built by diving in and figuring things out along the way, collect data, try a model, see what happens. This track forced a different order. FL-06 made me write the job to be done, the tools, five real eval cases, and the guardrails *before* a single instruction went into the agent. That felt slower at first. It wasn't. It meant that when I actually built the agent in FL-07, I already knew exactly what "working" meant, so every run against those eval cases was a real test, not a vibe check. I'll take that habit into whatever I build next, spec first, eval cases before code, decide what "done well" looks like before starting instead of after.

The three things I'm keeping, the ones I'd genuinely reuse on a completely different project:

First, writing eval cases before building. It's tempting to just start and see what happens, but writing down five specific test cases up front, before any code exists, forces you to actually define success instead of discovering it by accident. It also means failures are informative instead of just disappointing, when the ML Research Scout agent came back empty on four different topics, I could look at the eval cases and see clearly that the problem was a miscalibrated time window in the spec, not a broken agent.

Second, getting real external feedback and not defending the work. Sending my live portfolio to Syed Sarmad Shan Bukhari, my actual "one person" from Week 1, and later sending him the hardened version, and just listening instead of explaining myself away, was uncomfortable in a useful way. His feedback (deploy the model, resize the screenshots, explain what each screen actually does) was specific and correct in a way I couldn't have seen myself after staring at the same four pages for weeks.

Third, checking real evidence instead of eyeballing quality. I didn't know my portfolio's colors already passed accessibility contrast standards until I actually computed the ratios. I didn't know my profile photo was 638KB, wildly oversized for how it displayed, until I checked the actual file size. "Looks fine to me" and "I measured it and it's fine" turned out to be very different claims, and the second one is the one worth trusting.

What I'd build next: Basic Needs Justice, a system for Pakistani citizens to report and track exploitation around basic needs like groceries, petrol, and water pricing. It's a narrower, more honest starting slice of a bigger thing I actually care about, people losing out to whoever has more money or power, land disputes and case-tracking included, but named as future work instead of attempted all at once in a way that would guarantee nothing ships.

You came in wanting to learn some AI tools. You're leaving having shipped a real, working, documented thing, and knowing exactly how you'd start the next one.
